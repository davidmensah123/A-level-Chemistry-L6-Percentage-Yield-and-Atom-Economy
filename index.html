01 Percentage Yield

In chemical synthesis, the maximum mass of product predicted by stoichiometric calculations is the **theoretical yield**. In laboratory and industrial practice, the **actual yield** obtained after purification is almost always lower.

Percentage Yield Equation
\[\text{Percentage Yield} = \frac{\text{Actual Yield}}{\text{Theoretical Yield}} \times 100\%\]

Where actual yield and theoretical yield are measured in identical units (grams, kilograms, tonnes, or moles).

Why is it difficult to achieve \(100\%\) yield? (Examiner Favorites)
  • Incomplete Reaction: The reaction may be reversible and reach dynamic equilibrium before completion.
  • Mechanical Transfer Loss: Product is left on filter paper, beaker sides, glass funnels, or lost during washing and transferring.
  • Side Reactions: Competing side reactions can produce unwanted alternative by-products.
  • Gaseous Escape: Products that form as gas may escape before collection apparatus is sealed.

Higher-Tier Specification Application

Worked Example: Synthesis of Aspirin (2-Ethanoyloxybenzoic Acid)

Multi-Step Molar Yield

Problem: A student prepares a sample of aspirin (\(\text{C}_9\text{H}_8\text{O}_4\), \(M_r = 180.16\text{ g mol}^{-1}\)) by reacting \(6.90\text{ g}\) of salicylic acid (\(\text{C}_7\text{H}_6\text{O}_3\), \(M_r = 138.12\text{ g mol}^{-1}\)) with an excess of ethanoic anhydride (\((\text{CH}_3\text{CO})_2\text{O}\)) in the presence of an acid catalyst:

\[\text{C}_7\text{H}_6\text{O}_{3(s)} + (\text{CH}_3\text{CO})_2\text{O}_{(l)} \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+} \text{C}_9\text{H}_8\text{O}_{4(s)} + \text{CH}_3\text{COOH}_{(aq)}\]

After recrystallisation and drying to constant mass, the student isolates \(5.65\text{ g}\) of pure aspirin.
(a) Calculate the theoretical yield of aspirin in grams.
(b) Calculate the percentage yield of aspirin obtained, giving your answer to \(3\) significant figures.
(c) Suggest two practical reasons, other than incomplete reaction, why the student did not obtain a \(100\%\) yield.

Step 1: Calculate amount (in moles) of the limiting reactant (Salicylic Acid)

Since ethanoic anhydride is present in excess, the theoretical yield of product is determined solely by the starting mass of salicylic acid: \[n(\text{C}_7\text{H}_6\text{O}_3) = \frac{m}{M_r} = \frac{6.90\text{ g}}{138.12\text{ g mol}^{-1}} = 0.049957\text{ mol}\]

Step 2: Deduce theoretical moles of aspirin produced

From the balanced stoichiometric equation, the molar ratio between salicylic acid and aspirin is \(1 : 1\): \[n(\text{aspirin})_{\text{theoretical}} = n(\text{C}_7\text{H}_6\text{O}_3) = 0.049957\text{ mol}\]

Step 3: Calculate the maximum theoretical mass of aspirin

\[m(\text{aspirin})_{\text{theoretical}} = n \times M_r = 0.049957\text{ mol} \times 180.16\text{ g mol}^{-1} = \mathbf{9.000\text{ g}} \quad (\text{or } 9.00\text{ g to } 3\text{ s.f.})\]

Step 4: Compute the percentage yield

\[\% \text{ Yield} = \frac{\text{Actual Yield}}{\text{Theoretical Yield}} \times 100\% = \frac{5.65\text{ g}}{9.000\text{ g}} \times 100\% = \mathbf{62.8\%}\]

Step 5: Practical loss factors (Examiner criteria for part c)
  • Recrystallisation losses: Some aspirin remains dissolved in the cold solvent during vacuum filtration.
  • Transfer losses: Crystals remain on the filter paper, Buchner funnel, watch glass, or stirring rod during mechanical transfer.
  • Side reactions: Polymerisation or competing side reactions consume some salicylic acid to produce unwanted impurities.

02 Limiting Reagents & Reactant in Excess

The **limiting reagent** is the reactant that is completely consumed first in a reaction. It places a hard ceiling on the maximum amount of product that can be formed. The reactant remaining after the reaction ceases is in **excess**.

Step 1 • Convert to Moles

Calculate Starting Amounts

Convert masses or solution volumes of all starting reactants into moles using \(n = m/M\) or \(n = c \times V\).

Step 2 • Compare Mole Ratios

Check Stoichiometric Need

Use the balanced chemical equation to determine how many moles of reactant B are required to react with all of reactant A.

Step 3 • Identify Bottleneck

Base Product on Limiting

The reactant with fewer moles than stoichiometrically required is limiting. Base all product yield calculations solely on this reactant!

03 Atom Economy & Green Chemistry

While percentage yield measures practical conversion efficiency, **atom economy** measures the theoretical efficiency of a chemical reaction in terms of waste generation and raw material conservation.

Atom Economy Formula
\[\text{Atom Economy} = \frac{M_r \text{ of desired product}}{\text{Sum of } M_r \text{ of all products}} \times 100\%\]

Always use the balanced stoichiometric equation and multiply each \(M_r\) by its balancing coefficient!

Addition Route • 100% Atom Economy

Hydration of Ethene

\[\text{C}_2\text{H}_{4(g)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}_{(g)} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4} \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}_{(g)}\]

Desired Product \(M_r(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}) = 46.07\)
Total Products \(M_r = 46.07\)
\(\text{Atom Economy} = \frac{46.07}{46.07} \times 100\% = \mathbf{100\%}\)

All reactant atoms are incorporated into the single desired product. Zero chemical waste is produced.

Substitution / By-Product Route

Fermentation of Glucose

\[\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_{6(aq)} \xrightarrow{\text{yeast}} 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}_{(aq)} + 2\text{CO}_{2(g)}\]

Desired Product \(2 \times M_r(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}) = 2 \times 46.07 = 92.14\)
Total Products \(M_r = 92.14 + (2 \times 44.01) = 180.16\)
\(\text{Atom Economy} = \frac{92.14}{180.16} \times 100\% = \mathbf{51.1\%}\)

Nearly half the reactant mass (\(48.9\%\)) is wasted as gaseous \(\text{CO}_2\), unless captured commercially.

Why is High Atom Economy Crucial in Industrial Green Chemistry?

1. Reduces Waste Minimizes the quantity of hazardous chemical waste that requires costly storage, neutralizing, or disposal.
2. Conserves Resources Maximizes the utilization of finite, non-renewable raw fossil fuel feedstocks and precious catalysts.
3. Lowers Costs Reduces raw material procurement expenses and avoids steep regulatory environmental waste taxes.
4. Green Chemistry Underpins the UN Sustainable Development Goals for environmentally benign, atom-efficient chemical synthesis.
